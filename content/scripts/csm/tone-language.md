# Tone & Language Standards

The words underneath every other script. These are the phrases that decide whether a customer thinks they reached a company or a person.

## Say this {#say}

| Phrase | Why |
|---|---|
| "Great question." | Rewards them for asking rather than making them feel slow |
| "I'd be happy to help." | Willingness, not obligation |
| "Let me find out for you." | The correct answer when you don't know |
| "Here's what I recommend." | Positions you as the expert they called |
| "Absolutely." | Costs nothing, lands warm |

## Avoid this {#avoid}

| Phrase | What the customer hears |
|---|---|
| ❌ "That's not my department." | *You're my problem now.* Own the handoff instead — `sop.csm.role`. |
| ❌ "I don't know." | *Nobody here can help you.* Say "let me find out for you." |
| ❌ "Calm down." | Guaranteed escalation. Lower your own volume instead — `sop.csm.de-escalation`. |
| ❌ "That's our policy." | *The rule matters more than you do.* Explain the reason, or escalate. |
| ❌ "You should have…" | Blame. There is no version of this that helps. |

## The pattern underneath {#pattern}

Every phrase in the avoid column moves responsibility toward the customer. Every phrase in the say column keeps it with us.

That's the whole test. Before a sentence leaves your mouth on a hard call, ask which direction it moves the problem.

## "That's our policy" deserves special mention {#policy}

It is technically true and completely useless. If a policy exists, there's a reason for it — give the reason: *"The diagnostic covers the tech's time and a written price, and it comes off the repair if you go ahead."* If you can't explain the reason, that's a signal the policy needs revisiting, not a signal to hide behind it. Raise it at the monthly review — `governance.coaching`.

## Written communication {#written}

Same standards, plus: no emojis, no all-caps, full sentences, sign with your first name. See `sop.csm.communication` and `playbook.sms`.
