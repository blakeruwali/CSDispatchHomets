# Objection — "I'll try to fix it myself first"

## The response {#response}

> "I completely understand — a lot of homeowners like to try a quick fix first. Just so you know what you're walking into: with HVAC and plumbing, one small misstep can turn a $200 repair into a $2,000 replacement, and some work legally has to be done by a licensed professional. What I'd hate to see is you spend your Saturday on it and still need us Monday with a bigger problem. The easiest thing is to let a tech come out, diagnose it properly, and give you a written flat price before any work starts. If you approve the repair, the diagnostic is credited. Does morning or afternoon work better?"

This response does not insult the customer's ability. It respects the instinct, reframes the risk, and brings the conversation back to certainty and protection.

### Why this response works

- **It agrees first.** "I completely understand" removes the adversarial feel.
- **It reframes DIY from saving money to risking money.** The customer thinks they are avoiding a fee; the response shows they may be creating a larger bill.
- **It introduces legal/safety stakes without fear-mongering.** Some work requires a licensed tech — that is a fact, not a sales tactic.
- **It protects their time.** "Spend your Saturday and still need us Monday" speaks to the pain of wasted effort.
- **It ends with the same low-friction close.** Morning or afternoon is easier than "do you want to book?"

If they insist on trying first, use the soft hold:

> "Fair enough. While you're trying it, let me hold a slot for early next week — no obligation, and if you get it handled, just cancel. If not, you'll already have the appointment and won't be waiting another three days. Does morning or afternoon work better?"

## Why the customer is saying this {#why-saying}

"I'll try to fix it myself first" sounds like one objection, but it means several different things. Handling them the same way loses bookings.

| What they say | What they usually mean | How to handle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "I'll try to fix it myself first." (confident, specific) | They genuinely believe they can solve it — often from a YouTube video or past experience. | Acknowledge their skill, then focus on what a professional diagnosis protects them from. Don't argue; show the downside of guessing wrong. |
| "I want to see if I can avoid the diagnostic fee." | The fee is the real issue, not their ability. | Treat it as a price objection. Recap the value and the credit toward approved repair. |
| "My husband/brother/neighbor knows about this stuff." | They are delegating the decision to someone else, often to avoid feeling dumb. | Offer to send information the other person can review, and hold the slot while they check. |
| "I'll watch a video and see." | They underestimate the complexity or danger. | Gently name the specific risk for their system type — electrical, refrigerant, gas, water pressure — without being dramatic. |
| "Let me try a reset/cleaning first." | They want a low-effort fix before calling. | Approve the safe attempt, set a time boundary, and book a follow-up slot in case it doesn't hold. |
| "I've done this before." | Pride and prior competence. | Respect it, then point to the difference between a quick fix and a full-system diagnosis that prevents repeat failure. |
| "I don't want some salesman in my house." | Trust issue disguised as DIY. | Emphasize licensed technician, diagnostic-only visit, written flat price, and no-pressure process. |

The goal is not to convince them they are helpless. The goal is to convince them that a professional diagnosis is the smarter first step before money, time, or safety is risked.

## Why it works {#why}

DIY objections are usually about control, not competence. The customer wants to feel like they tried something before spending money. If you mock that instinct or push too hard, you become the pushy company that talked down to them.

The winning response:

1. **Honors their intent.** Trying first is rational.
2. **Names the hidden risk.** A small mistake on an HVAC or plumbing system can escalate fast.
3. **Offers a no-lose path.** The diagnostic gives certainty; if they still want to DIY after, they have a written diagnosis to work from.
4. **Doesn't argue facts.** You don't need to prove they can't do it. You need to prove that diagnosing first is worth it either way.

## How to handle it {#execution}

1. **Agree without endorsing.** "I completely understand — a lot of people try that first."
2. **Name the risk for their system.** Electrical, refrigerant, gas, water damage, warranty void — pick the relevant one.
3. **Protect their time and money.** Frame the diagnostic as insurance against a bigger bill or wasted weekend.
4. **Offer the soft hold.** If they still want to try, hold a slot so they do not lose days if it fails.
5. **Close on a small choice.** "Does morning or afternoon work better?"

## Scenario responses {#scenarios}

### "I'll watch a YouTube video first"

> "YouTube is great for a lot of things. The tricky part with HVAC is that the symptom you see is often not the actual cause — a frozen coil can be low refrigerant, a failing blower, or a duct issue. A tech figures out the real cause in about 30 minutes and gives you a written flat price. That way if you do want to tackle it yourself, you at least know what you're actually fixing. Does morning or afternoon work better?"

### "My husband can probably fix it"

> "That's great — it's nice to have someone handy. If he wants to take a look, no problem at all. While he's looking, let me hold a slot for tomorrow or the next day — no obligation, and if he gets it handled, you can cancel. If not, you won't be stuck waiting. Does morning or afternoon work better?"

### "I just need to reset it"

> "A reset is always worth a shot. If it comes right back on, that's usually a sign of a deeper issue. Here's what I'd suggest: try the reset, and if the problem returns within the next day or two, let a tech diagnose it properly. I can hold a slot for you now — no obligation — so you have it if needed. Does morning or afternoon work better?"

### "I want to try a part I found online"

> "I get it — parts online can look a lot cheaper. The risk is ordering the wrong part or swapping something that isn't actually failed. A misdiagnosed part swap can void your warranty and turn a repair into a replacement. A tech tells you exactly what's failed before you spend money on parts. Does morning or afternoon work better?"

### "I don't want a salesman pressuring me"

> "Totally fair. This isn't a sales visit — it's a diagnostic. A licensed tech comes out, finds what's wrong, and gives you a written flat price. If you want to fix it yourself after that, you'll at least know exactly what the problem is. No pressure, no hidden fees. Does morning or afternoon work better?"

### "I've fixed these before"

> "Then you know how these systems can be. The difference this time is getting a full diagnosis before you start taking things apart. That way if it's a simple fix, you know it. And if it's something bigger, you know that too before the weekend is gone. Does morning or afternoon work better?"

## Worked example — confident DIYer {#example-confident}

**Caller:** "I think I can handle this myself. I'll try a few things first and call back if I need you."

**CSM:** "I completely understand — a lot of homeowners like to try a quick fix first. What kind of system is it?"

**Caller:** "It's a gas furnace. The igniter looks like it might be bad."

**CSM:** "That could definitely be it. Just so you have the full picture before you start: with a gas furnace, a small mistake on the ignition or gas valve side can be a safety issue, and sometimes what looks like a bad igniter is actually a failing flame sensor or a draft motor issue. A tech can diagnose it in about 30 minutes and give you a written flat price. If it's the igniter and you still want to swap it yourself, at least you'll know for sure. Does morning or afternoon work better?"

## Worked example — fee disguised as DIY {#example-fee}

**Caller:** "I'm going to try a couple things before I pay someone to come out."

**CSM:** "Makes sense. Just so you know, the {{price:diagnostic_residential}} covers the full diagnosis — a licensed tech checks the whole system, finds the real cause, and gives you a written flat price before any work starts. If you approve the repair that day, the diagnostic gets credited to the work. So either way, you're not paying for a guess. Does morning or afternoon work better?"

## Worked example — soft hold after DIY attempt {#example-hold}

**Caller:** "I really want to try it this weekend. If it doesn't work, I'll call you Monday."

**CSM:** "Fair enough. While you're trying it, let me hold a slot for Monday — no obligation, and if you get it handled, just cancel. If not, you won't be waiting another three days for an opening. Does morning or afternoon work better?"

## What not to do {#avoid}

- ❌ Don't tell the customer they are not capable of fixing it.
- ❌ Don't mock YouTube, forums, or online parts.
- ❌ Don't invent dangers or use scare tactics.
- ❌ Don't accept "I'll call you back Monday" without at least offering to hold the slot.
- ❌ Don't debate whether DIY is ever a good idea. Keep it specific to this system and this symptom.
- ❌ Don't drop the price as a bribe. The value is certainty and protection, not a discount.

## QA scoring {#scoring}

- **0** = accepted the DIY objection without testing the real concern or holding a slot.
- **1** = acknowledged the DIY attempt but left the call without a scheduled or held window.
- **2** = agreed respectfully, named the relevant risk, offered the soft hold, and secured a specific slot.
