# The Seven Objections

> **An objection is information, not resistance. Almost every one of these is a different question wearing the same words — your job is to find which question it is before you answer.**

Each section: why they are really hesitating, what to say, and what never to say. The "never" lists are as important as the scripts.

## 1. "That's too expensive" {#expensive}

**Why they say it.** Sticker shock — they are comparing to a repair, not to another install. They do not yet see the total value. They have not been shown monthly. They may have unrealistic expectations from online pricing.

**Acknowledge, then reframe:**

> "I completely understand — it's a significant investment. Let me break down what you're actually getting."

> "When we look at this monthly, the Better package comes out to about $X/month, and that includes your warranty, membership, and everything."

**Cost of inaction:**

> "If we look at what you've spent on repairs the last two years — $[X] — plus the higher bills on a [Y] SEER system, the new system actually saves you money over five years."

**Then offer flexibility:** 0% financing where it applies (`sop.sales.financing`).

**Never:** drop the price immediately · say "I can talk to my manager" · apologise for the price · compare yourself to the cheapest competitor.

## 2. "I need to think about it" {#think}

**This is almost never the real objection.** It is usually price, spouse, or wanting other quotes. Find the real one and answer *that*.

**Isolate it:**

> "Absolutely — it's a big decision. Can I ask: is it the system itself you're unsure about, or the investment?"

**Then address what they said.** Investment → financing. System → re-tie the features to the problems they named.

**Set the follow-up before you leave:**

> "Can I check in with you [Thursday]? I'll leave the proposal so you can review it with [spouse]."

**Never:** pressure them (*"What's there to think about?"*) · leave without a follow-up date · accept it at face value without one gentle probe · make them feel guilty for not deciding today.

## 3. "I want to get a few more quotes" {#quotes}

**Why they say it.** They feel responsible to comparison shop. Someone told them to always get three. They are not yet sold on *you*. Or they want leverage.

**Agree first — genuinely:**

> "That's smart — I'd do the same thing. Can I share something that will help you compare apples to apples?"

**Then make yourself the expert:**

> "When you get other quotes, make sure they include the same SEER rating, the same warranty terms, install details, and whether they're pulling a permit. A lot of companies leave things out to look cheaper."

**Leave the comparison sheet.** One page: SEER2 rating · warranty terms · who does the install · permit included · disposal of old equipment · start-up testing · membership included. **This wins jobs even when they leave the house undecided**, because every other quote now gets measured against our checklist.

**Never:** badmouth competitors · drop your price to match someone they have not even called · act offended or desperate · skip the follow-up.

## 4. "I need to talk to my spouse" {#spouse}

**Usually legitimate.** Major purchases are joint decisions. Sometimes it is a polite brush-off. Sometimes they are sold and know their partner will push back on price.

**Validate, then help them sell it:**

> "Completely understand — this is a family decision. Would it help if I put together a simple summary you can share with [name]?"

> "The key things to share: it solves [X problem], the monthly is $[Y], and it includes the full warranty and membership. I'll put it on one page."

**Offer to come back:**

> "Or I'm happy to come back when you're both available. Sometimes it's easier when I can answer questions directly."

**Get the micro-commitment:**

> "In the meantime, which option were *you* leaning toward?"

**Prevent it entirely** by asking at the start of the visit who else is involved in the decision (`sop.sales.arrival`).

**Never:** say *"Can you call them now?"* · dismiss it (*"You don't need permission"*) · leave without a follow-up plan · leave without a **written** proposal — the absent partner needs something to actually read.

## 5. "Can't I just repair it?" {#repair}

**Why they say it.** They do not see the long-term cost of repeated repairs. Emotional attachment to the system. Fear of the unknown.

**Agree partially — and mean it:**

> "You could repair it, and I can do that for you. The repair would be about $[X]. I just want to make sure you have the full picture first."

**Show the math:**

> "Your system is [X] years old, average lifespan is 15–20. You've spent $[Y] on repairs in the last [Z] years."

**The $5,000 rule:** if repair cost × age of system exceeds $5,000, replacement is usually the better financial decision. A $800 repair on a 12-year-old system is $9,600 — well past it.

**Then let them choose:**

> "I can do the repair today, no problem. Or we can look at replacement with a full warranty. What feels right for you?"

**Never:** refuse to do the repair · make them feel stupid for wanting one · guarantee the repair will fail — you do not know that · forget to present repair **and** replace side by side.

## 6. "I saw this unit cheaper online" {#online}

**They did their homework.** Respect it.

> "You're right — you can find the equipment cheaper online. Here's the important difference."

**Break down the real cost.** The online price is the box. Ours includes professional installation, permits, disposal of the old equipment, start-up and testing, warranty registration, and the labor guarantee.

**The warranty catch most people do not know:**

> "Manufacturers require professional installation for the warranty to be valid. Buy online, hire a handyman, and the manufacturer warranty is void."

**The 60/40 rule — your strongest argument here.** Equipment is about 40% of how a system performs; installation quality is the other 60%. Refrigerant charge, duct connections, electrical, airflow testing. A good unit installed badly underperforms, fails early, and voids warranties.

**Never:** say *"you get what you pay for"* · dismiss their research · try to match the online price · argue.

## 7. "I've been burned by contractors before" {#burned}

**Their guard is up and it is not about you.** Do not take it personally, and do not try to talk them out of the feeling.

**Acknowledge it plainly:**

> "I'm sorry you went through that — it happens too often in this industry. That's exactly why we do things differently."

**Then prove it rather than claim it:**

- Reviews — pull them up on your phone and show them. *"Here's what homeowners in your area are saying."*
- Licensed, insured, permits pulled on every job.
- Your direct number: *"If anything goes wrong, you call me."*
- **Transparency:** *"I showed you exactly what I found, with photos. The proposal is itemized — no hidden fees."*
- The labor warranty: *"If anything isn't right, we come back and fix it. No charge."*

**Never:** badmouth the previous contractor, even when they deserve it · dismiss the concern (*"We're not like them"*) · get offended · over-promise to compensate. **Promising more than we deliver is how they got here in the first place.**
