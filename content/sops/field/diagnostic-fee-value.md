# Presenting the Diagnostic Fee — Value When the Customer Doesn't Want to Pay

> **The fee is not the problem. The customer not knowing what the fee buys is the problem. Your job is to make the value visible before the number comes up.**

## When this applies {#applies}

Every diagnostic visit, from the moment you arrive to the moment the invoice is signed. It applies with full force when the customer says any version of:

- "I already paid to have you come out — why is there another charge?"
- "The other company doesn't charge a diagnostic."
- "I already know what's wrong, I just need it fixed."
- "I'm not paying $199 just for you to look at it."

The CSM already set the fee on the phone (`sop.csm.diagnostic-fee`). You are not re-selling it — you are *delivering* it so visibly that the customer feels they got more than they paid for.

## Why the customer is saying this {#why}

| What they say | What they usually mean |
|---|---|
| "I just want it fixed" | They think the diagnosis is a formality standing between them and the repair |
| "The other company was free" | They got a guess over the phone or a free look that ended in a surprise bill, and they assume everyone works that way |
| "I already know what's wrong" | They read a forum or watched a video and want you to skip to swapping the part |
| "That's too much" | Nobody has ever shown them what a real diagnosis includes, so the number has nothing attached to it |

Almost none of these customers are truly refusing to pay. They are refusing to pay for *something they can't see*. Show them the work and the objection usually dissolves on its own.

## The execution ladder {#execution}

1. **Set the expectation at the door, before you touch anything.** One sentence: what you're going to do, what they'll have at the end. This is where the value gets planted — not at invoice time.
2. **Narrate the work as you do it.** Say what you're measuring and why, in plain language, as you go. A silent technician looks like a $199 walk-around. A technician who says "I'm checking static pressure because low airflow is the number-one cause of what you're seeing" looks like a professional earning a fee.
3. **Show the numbers.** Readings, photos, the data plate. Customers believe what they can see. "Your supply air is 48 degrees and it should be around 55 — here's the reading" lands completely differently than "it's low."
4. **Deliver the written report and the written price.** The fee bought a named cause, the readings that prove it, and a flat written price (`sop.field.diagnostics`). Hand it over like the product it is — because it is the product.
5. **State the credit plainly.** If they approve the repair today, the diagnostic is credited toward it (`reference.guarantees`). Say it without apology or hesitation.
6. **If they decline, close with a straight back.** The service was rendered. The fee stands. You did the work, they have the report, and the report is theirs to keep.

## What you say {#verbatim}

**At the door:**

> "Here's how this works. I'm going to run a full diagnostic on the system — not just the part you think is wrong, the whole system, because the symptom and the cause are often two different things. When I'm done you'll have a written report with exactly what's wrong, the readings that prove it, and a flat written price to fix it. If you go ahead with the repair, the diagnostic is credited toward it. Fair enough?"

**While working:**

> "I'm measuring the temperature split across the coil — this tells me whether the system is actually moving heat the way it should."
>
> "This reading is the one that matters for your complaint. Normal is around X. Yours is Y. That's the problem, and now I can prove it instead of guessing."

**Presenting the result:**

> "Here's your report. This is what's wrong, these are the numbers that show it, and this is the flat price to fix it — that number doesn't change once we start. And if you approve the repair today, the diagnostic you paid is credited toward it, so it doesn't cost you anything extra."

**When they push back on the fee:**

> "I understand — nobody loves a fee. Here's what it actually got you: a licensed technician at your door, a full check of the entire system instead of a guess at one part, and a written price you can hold me to. The free look from the other company is how people end up paying for the same repair twice. You paid for certainty, and that's what you have."

**When they decline the repair:**

> "No problem — the report is yours to keep. It documents exactly what's wrong and what it takes to fix it, so whether you call us back or get a second opinion, you're starting from facts instead of a guess."

## Worked example {#example}

**The situation.** Mrs. Okafor's AC is blowing warm. On the phone she told the CSM "my husband says it just needs freon." When you arrive she says: "He already told me what's wrong. I'm not paying $199 for you to tell me the same thing."

**Weak handling:** "Well, I still have to charge the diagnostic, it's company policy." — The fee is now a toll, not a service. She pays it resentfully or argues you down, and the visit starts adversarial.

**Perfect execution:**

> "He might be right — low refrigerant does cause warm air. But here's the thing: refrigerant doesn't get used up. If it's low, it leaked, and adding more without finding the leak means you're buying the same refrigerant again in three months. What the diagnostic gets you is the actual answer — I'll measure the pressures, check the coil, and if it is low, I'll find out why. You'll have it in writing with a flat price. And if you approve the repair, the diagnostic is credited toward it, so it costs you nothing extra to know for sure."

She agrees. You run the diagnostic, find a failing capacitor — not low refrigerant at all — and show her the reading. The repair is a fraction of what a refrigerant misdiagnosis would have cost her. The fee just saved her money, and she knows it.

## Failure branches & hard rules {#hard-rules}

- **Never apologize for the fee, discount it at the door, or waive it to end an awkward conversation.** The fee is the product. Membership status is the only legitimate adjustment, and it comes from the system, not from you (`sop.field.membership`).
- **Never skip the diagnosis because the customer "already knows."** If they're right, you've confirmed it and earned the repair. If they're wrong — and they often are — you've just saved them from buying the wrong part. Either way the fee was worth it, and it's your job to say so.
- **Never let the invoice be the first time the fee's value comes up.** If the customer is surprised at the end, the door conversation failed.
- **If the customer refuses the fee outright at the door,** do not start work. Call Dispatch. The office re-confirms the booking terms — you do not negotiate them on the doorstep.
- **If the unit is not repairable, the diagnostic is still charged** — and it is credited if they buy the replacement (`sop.field.forms`). Say both halves.

## QA scoring {#qa}

- **0 — Failed:** Fee waived or discounted at the door without membership basis; work started after the customer refused the fee; no written report or written price delivered; customer surprised by the charge at invoice time.
- **1 — Partial:** Fee collected and report delivered, but the value was never presented — no door expectation, no narrated findings, credit not mentioned or mentioned apologetically.
- **2 — Perfect:** Door expectation set, work narrated in plain language, numbers shown, written report and flat price delivered, credit stated confidently, and any pushback answered with value instead of apology.
