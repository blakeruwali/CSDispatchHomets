# Presenting the Investment

> **Say the number, then stop. The pause is the technique — filling it signals you think the price is too high, and the customer will agree with you.**

## Language {#language}

Small changes, and they are not cosmetic. The left column frames a cost; the right column frames a decision.

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| "The price is…" | "The investment for this option is…" |
| "It costs…" | "For as low as $X/month, you get…" |
| "The cheapest option…" | "The most affordable option…" |
| "You need to replace…" | "Based on what I found, I'd recommend…" |
| "This is expensive, but…" | "This is the premium package — here's why homeowners choose it…" |
| "Do you want to buy it?" | "Let me get the paperwork started for you" |

**None of this makes an expensive thing cheap.** It stops you apologising for a price that is fair, which is a different problem and a more common one.

## Then be quiet {#silence}

Present the number and stop. Do not justify, do not soften, do not add a discount nobody asked for. Techs lose more margin in the four seconds after the price than anywhere else in the visit.

If the silence goes long, the next words are still theirs. Wait.

## Never apologise for the price {#never-apologise}

- **Never drop the price on the spot.** It devalues everything you just presented and tells them the first number was not real.
- **Never say "let me talk to my manager."** It makes you powerless in a conversation where they need you to be the authority.
- **Never compare yourself to the cheapest competitor.**

If the price is genuinely wrong for the scope, that is an estimating problem to fix properly, not to discount around in the kitchen.
